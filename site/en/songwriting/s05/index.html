Four steps to setting lyrics to music

ReadSay the lyrics out loud
RhythmMark lengths and accents
PitchFollow the words' tones
MotifRepetition and sequence

Composing directly

Start from a motif, a set of chords or a backing, finish the melody first, then write the words. The melody is free, but when you add words later, watch the characters' tones.

Music set to existing lyrics

Write music following the lyrics' sections, syllable counts and emotions. The verse has more words and denser notes; the chorus has fewer words and more long notes, giving the emotion room to stretch.

BuildIntervals and scales

The pentatonic scale: drop 4 and 7, and the melody takes on an Eastern colour at once

Major scale

1 2 3 4 5 6 7: bright and stable, the basis of most pop songs.

Pentatonic scale

1 2 3 5 6: the pentatonic scale leaves out 4 and 7. Practise short phrases with these five notes first, then try them over chords.

Blues scale

1 ♭3 4 ♯4 5 ♭7: lazy and tense, common in R&B and rock.

BuildElements of melodic development

Steps are smooth; leaps have tension

Steps

Moving one neighbouring note at a time, such as 1 2 3 2 1. Smooth and speech-like, good for telling the story in the verse.

Leaps

A big move of a third or more, such as 1 5 1̇. Creates surprise and an outburst of emotion, good for the start of a chorus.

After a leap, a melody often steps back the other way to fill the gap. Listen to the chorus of 〈我願意〉 and notice how the melody of 「我願意為你」 pushes upward again and again.

BuildMelodic contour

Four common melodic shapes

Going up

The emotion rises gradually; common in the pre-chorus

Coming down

Letting go, sighing; common at the end of a phrase

Arch

Rises then falls, the most complete kind of sentence

Wave

Rises and falls within a small range, good for storytelling in the verse

Verse and chorus melodies need contrast: the verse sits in the low-to-middle range and moves in waves; the chorus moves higher and opens with an arch or a leap. The clearer the contrast, the more the chorus feels like a release.

Turn01:30–02:30 Chords and chord progressions

Chord families, each with its own job

FamilyDegreeJob
Tonic1 (6 as a substitute chord)Start and finish, bringing a sense of stability
Subdominant4 (2 as a substitute chord)Placed before the dominant, the first step away from home
Dominant5Placed before the final tonic, creating the tension of wanting to go home

How chords are built

Three notes are just right, and four or five aren't too many. A C chord is 1 3 5; G7 is 5 7 2 4.

The most common loop: 16451

Start on the tonic, pass through the subdominant and dominant chords, and return to the tonic. C → Am → F → G → C.

What chords do

In C major, which chord most wants to "go home" and usually comes just before the end?

  1. C
  2. Am
  3. G
  4. F
G is the dominant of C major (degree 5). It sounds full of tension and most wants to return to the tonic, C.

Which progression is "C → G → Am → F"?

  1. 1564
  2. 1645
  3. 4536
  4. 6415
C is 1, G is 5, Am is 6 and F is 4: the 1564 known as the all-purpose progression.

TurnWays to capture ideas

Melodies vanish fast, so record them first

  • Phone recording: the fastest way; humming or whistling both work
  • Numbered notation: write down pitches and lengths so they're easy to revise later
  • Chord chart: mark the chords above the lyrics
  • Melody bank: sort by mood or tempo so you can find it right away

One of the three goals of writing: always record it. A melody you forget usually doesn't come back.
