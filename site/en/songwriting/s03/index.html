Ci poems were made to be sung

Song ci were written to the tune of a "cipai", and 〈江城子〉 is the name of one. Lyrics from a thousand years ago can still be set to new music today and become a pop song.

Observe

Long and short lines alternate and the line endings rhyme (茫, 量, 忘, 涼, 霜, 鄉, 妝, 行, 岡), so it reads like a melody.

The stricter the metre, the more refined the words, but not necessarily the more singable. Pop lyrics sit between poetry and speech: they keep the rhythm, and they keep the naturalness of talk.

Song ci

Carried on the spirit of Han and Wei yuefu, absorbed the influence of music from the northern peoples, and grew out of folk song. Ci could be called poetry set free, with no limit on characters or lines.

Yuan qu

A new form from the Jin and Yuan dynasties onward, evolved from ci. It blended the street songs of the north with foreign tunes, combining metre and freedom.

BuildWhat poems, ci and qu are like

Seeing classical verse again in the language of pop music

Poems

Strict metre, careful about parallelism and paired lines, with less thought for singability.

Words

Loose form, allowing changes in characters and lines. It's written much like "writing lyrics" in pop.

Music

Inherits the form of ci and adds filler characters, making it the most singable. It's written much like "setting lyrics" in pop.

Sanqu ≈ single

A standalone song that can be released on its own.

Opera songs ≈ album, opera

Several songs strung into one complete story, like a concept album or a musical.

BuildWriting vs. setting lyrics

Writing lyrics makes standalone words; setting lyrics makes words for a tune

Writing lyrics

  • Written following a pop song's form
  • The words can be read on their own and aren't necessarily singable
  • Groove and sound need extra thought when the music is written

Setting lyrics

  • Written following the form and sounds, making it more singable
  • Takes the melody's contour and pitch into account to reduce clashes between words and music
  • Placing the hook is key, echoing the title where needed

Your song will use both: today and next lesson you write lyrics first, then after setting music in Lessons 5 and 6 you go back and revise the words to the melody, which is setting lyrics.

BuildSingability and tones

Mandarin has four tones, and the melody should follow each character's direction

TonePitch directionExample
1st toneHigh and level天、心、風
2nd toneRising from low來、情、愁
3rd toneDown then up你、想、遠
4th toneFalling from high愛、夢、淚

Tone clash

When the melody moves against a character's tone, listeners easily misunderstand. Put 「淚」 (4th tone, "tears") on a rising melody, for instance, and it's easily heard as 「雷」 (2nd tone, "thunder").

Speak the lyrics first, then hum the melody, and compare which way each goes. Words on long notes and strong beats matter most; try to make their tones match the melody.

TurnRhyme analysis: the teacher's songs

Use the rhyme checker to analyse the rhyme strategy of 〈微型鳳凰〉

  • Each line of the original is long, so it's split into short lines where the singer pauses
  • Watch where the ㄤ rhyme appears and work out the rhyme strategy
  • Find the lines that don't rhyme and explain what they do in the melody

The pre-chorus and chorus nearly all rhyme on ㄤ: 徬徨, 方向, 倔強, 鳳凰, 往前闖, 翅膀. 「別害怕」 and 「就算逆著風」 are short pauses mid-line where the melody takes a breath, and the next line returns to ㄤ, so the whole passage is close to one rhyme throughout.

TurnPoint of view

Who's speaking, and to whom

First person

"I" speaks directly. It moves people most easily and is the most common view in pop lyrics.

Second person

Speaking to "you", like a letter; the 「寫信告訴我」 ("write and tell me") of 〈聽海〉 uses this voice.

Third person

An onlooker tells "his" or "her" story, keeping a distance and leaving room for the listener's imagination.

Don't waver: if the person and stance keep changing within one song, listeners can't find who's speaking. The planning card for 〈十年〉 reads "first-person stance, telling a story in first, second and third person": the speaker is always "I".
