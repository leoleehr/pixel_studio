Build00:20–00:55 Where the piano came from

Pianoforte: an instrument that can play "soft" and "loud"

Renaissance · Baroque

Harpsichord

The harpsichord plucks its strings, so the volume can't change with your touch.

1709

Cristofori

The Italian Bartolomeo Cristofori took the harpsichord as a model and switched to hammers striking the strings, making an early piano "with soft and loud".

Where the name comes from

Piano + Forte

The full name is pianoforte, "soft-loud". For convenience the forte, meaning loud, was dropped, leaving just piano.

Late 18th century

King of instruments

With its wide range and the ability to play melody and harmony together, it became the most important instrument for composing and music education.

BuildHow it's built and makes sound

Press a key and a hammer strikes the strings

  • In principle it's a struck string instrument; because you play it through a keyboard, it's classed as a keyboard instrument
  • 88 keys, a range from A0 to C8, over seven octaves, tuned in twelve-tone equal temperament
  • Over two hundred strings inside, with an iron frame taking their pull
  • Let go of a key and the damper stops the string vibrating straight away

BuildKinds of piano

From concert hall to bedroom: the keyboard family

Grand piano

Grand Piano

Strings lie horizontally and the hammers strike from below; a responsive touch, standard for concerts.

Upright piano

Upright Piano

Strings stand vertically and the hammers strike from the front; compact, and most common in homes and classrooms.

Digital piano

Digital Piano

Samples a real piano's sound; you can practise with headphones and connect to a computer over MIDI.

Keyboards, synthesizers

Keyboard · Synthesizer

49 to 88 keys, with lots of built-in sounds and rhythms; light and easy to carry.

There are also silent pianos you can practise on with headphones, player pianos, and organs with two manuals plus a pedalboard. For a beginner, an instrument with at least 61 touch-sensitive keys is best.

BuildClassical, pop and jazz

Pop piano is the foundation for jazz piano

Classical piano

Played from staff notation, with an emphasis on sight-reading and fine technical interpretation.

Pop piano

Accompanying freely from chord symbols, adding ornaments and fills, and pushing and laying back with syncopation for a pop feel.

Jazz piano

Mostly improvised, with seventh chords and walking bass; play a song a hundred times and it's different every time.

Three elementsPop pianoJazz piano
HarmonyMore consonant triadsDissonant seventh chords
MelodyOrnaments and syncopation added to the originalImprovised variations

Turn01:05–01:40 Sitting and hand shape

Sit steady and relaxed, fingers as if holding a ball

  • Sit on the front half of the bench, centred on middle C, feet flat on the floor
  • Bench height: elbows roughly level with the keys
  • Shoulders relaxed, upper arms hanging naturally, wrists neither sagging nor raised
  • Fingers curved, playing with the fingertips; the thumb plays on its side

Checklist

Loose shoulders, level elbows, straight wrists, round fingers

If your shoulders or wrists feel tight while playing, stop, shake out your hands and set your hand shape again. Playing slowly is fine; tension is what you most need to avoid.

TurnFinger numbers

The thumb is 1 and the little finger 5, on both hands

This course writes accompaniment as "L5 L3 L2 L1" and "R1 R2 R5": L is the left hand, R the right, and the numbers are fingers.

TurnPedals

Three pedals, each with a job

Right

Sustain pedal

Pressing it lifts the dampers off the strings, so the sound keeps going after you let go of the keys. It's the one pop piano uses most; change the pedal when you change chords.

Left

Soft pedal

Makes the sound quieter and softer. On a grand it shifts the hammers; on an upright it moves the hammers closer to the strings.

Middle

Sostenuto or practice pedal

On a grand it sustains only certain notes; on an upright it's often a mute for quiet practice.

Right after you press the chord, lift the pedal and press it again, so the new chord is sustained and the old one is cut off. Remember: play, lift, press.

Myth or fact?

You have to master classical piano before you can play pop
MythPop piano starts from chords, so you can begin from zero; classical technique is a great help, though.
The thinner the string, the lower the note
MythThe thinner and shorter the string, the higher the note; bass strings are thick and long and wrapped in copper wire.
A piano needs regular tuning
FactStrings stretch over time and go out of tune; tune at least once a year.
Holding the sustain pedal down the whole time sounds best
MythDifferent chords blur together into mud; change the pedal when you change chords.
A piano doesn't like damp, or too dry either
FactThe right environment is about 20°C with 50–60% humidity, out of direct sunlight.
You have to press hard for a good sound
MythLetting the arm's weight fall naturally is stronger than pushing the keys, and won't hurt you.
