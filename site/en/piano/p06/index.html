Finger-position notation: writing accompaniment by where the fingers sit

  • L is the left hand, R the right, and the numbers are fingers
  • Left hand L5 L3 L2 L1 plays root, third, fifth and the root an octave up
  • Right hand R1 R2 R5 carries on upward: third, fifth, root
  • When the chord changes, the finger positions stay the same and the whole hand moves to the new root
BuildRhythm style (1): slow soul

Slow soul: the most common rhythm in today's pop

PatternNumbered notation (C chord)Finger positionsFeatures
(1)1̣ 5̣ 1 3 5 －L5 L2 L1 R1 R2Goes up and holds, leaving room for the melody
(2)1̣ 5̣ 1 3 5 1̇ 5 3L5 L2 L1 R1 R2 R5 R2 R1Up and back down, flowing eighth notes
(3)（1 3 5）× 4L5＋L3＋L2Block chords, steady and strong
(4)1̣ 5̣ 1 5̣ 3 5̣ 1 5̣L5 L2 L1 L2 R1 L2 L1 L2The fifth swings back and forth like a pendulum

You can mix the four patterns: use (1) in the verse to leave space, and switch to (2) or (4) in the chorus to lift the mood.

Myth or fact?

The more notes in the accompaniment, the better it sounds
MythAn accompaniment that's too full drowns the melody and voice; leaving space is a skill too.
When the chord changes, the accompaniment's finger positions stay the same
FactThe whole hand shifts to the new root and you play the same finger-position pattern.
Songs in triple time are always slow
MythTime signature has nothing to do with tempo; fast waltzes are common too.
The left-hand accompaniment should be quieter than the right hand
FactThe melody is the star and the accompaniment the stage; a lighter left hand lets the melody float above.
You can only use the pedal once you've learned everything else
MythBroken chords sound best with the sustain pedal; just remember to change the pedal with each chord.
A song can only use one accompaniment
MythUsing different accompaniments for the verse and chorus is common in pop piano.
