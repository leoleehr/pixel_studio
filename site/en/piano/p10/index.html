OpenListening to bass lines

Listen only to the lowest sound: it keeps going down

In Canon, the bass moves down a step every two beats, like walking down a long staircase. Slash chords are how we write that kind of bass.

Build00:20–00:55 Slash chords

Chord on the left of the slash, bass on the right

C/E

C chord, E in the bass

The right hand plays C E G and the left plays E. It's C in first inversion, with the third in the bass.

G/B

G chord, B in the bass

The right hand plays G B D and the left plays B, a half step down from C.

Am/G

Am chord, G in the bass

The bass isn't in the chord; it's a "non-chord bass" that keeps the bass line moving down.

Say it: C over E, G over B. Also called a slash chord. The left hand plays only the bass to the right of the slash and the right hand plays the chord as usual.

BuildConnecting roots

Two neighbouring bass notes should ideally be just one step apart

Original chordCGAmEmFCFG
Original bassCGAEFCFG
Connected rootsCG/BAmEm/GFC/EFG
New bassCBAGFEFG

The bass goes from jumping around to a scale walking down C B A G F E. The chord functions don't change, but it sounds more connected and has more direction.

Myth or fact?

Using the same progression is plagiarism
MythProgressions are shared material; what makes a song its own is the melody and lyrics.
A slash chord's bass is always a chord tone
MythThe bass notes of Am/G and C/B aren't in the chord; they're there to make the bass line move by step.
C/E is C in first inversion
FactThe lowest note is the third, E, and the notes don't change.
2-5-1 is only used in jazz
MythPop choruses often end with Dm → G → C back to the tonic too.
Remember the degrees and a progression still works after changing key
FactThe Canon progression in G major is G D Em Bm C G C D.
Bass lines can only go down
MythC → C/E → F → G goes up just as smoothly, with a sense of moving forward.
