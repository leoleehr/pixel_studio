OpenThink about it

With lungs the same size, some people last 10 seconds and others 30. Why?

What do you think the reason is? Vote by raising hands; the teacher counts

Lung capacity does vary from person to person, but the gaps you see in class mostly come from how you breathe in and how you control the exhale:

Breathe into the top of your chest and the air runs out fast; breathe all the way round your waist and belly, then push it out slowly from the belly, and the time stretches. We'll check it with the post-test before class ends.

Build2. Why listening matters

Hear the pitch clearly to sing it accurately

Pitch: being "tone-deaf" usually means the ears haven't been trained yet; the voice itself is fine.

Tone: recording yourself and listening back again and again is the most direct way to train your ear for sound.

Listen, think, then sing: play the target note → hum it once in your head → then sing it and see whether the needle lands in the green zone.

BuildFor guitarists

Sitting to play and sing

  • Sit on the front third of the chair with your pelvis settled
  • Keep the guitar close to your body and don't hunch forward
  • Music stand at eye level
  • Practise the chords until you don't need to look at your hands, then start playing and singing

Why sitting posture matters

Guitarists tend to look down at the fretboard.
Once the neck bends, the throat gets squeezed too,
high notes get harder and the breath gets shorter.

Build4. Caring for your voice

Myth or fact? Guess with a show of hands, then flip the card

When your throat is dry, iced water feels best
MythIced water makes the throat muscles tighten; warm water keeps the vocal folds moist.
Whispering lets your vocal folds rest
MythWhispering actually tenses the vocal folds; the real rest is to stop talking.
Drinking milk before singing soothes the throat
MythMilk and chocolate tend to cause phlegm; avoid them for three hours before performing.
Drink 1,500 to 2,000 ml of warm water a day
FactMoist membranes let the vocal folds vibrate freely; drink a little at a time, often.
When your throat feels stuck, give a hard cough
MythSwallow instead; a hard cough slams the vocal folds together.
Being hoarse the day after karaoke is normal, so just ignore it
MythYou need rest and water. If hoarseness lasts more than two weeks, see an ENT doctor.
A drink now and then is fine, even around a performance
MythAlcohol makes the vocal folds swell with blood; avoid it before and after singing.
Pause intense practice when you have a cold
FactForcing yourself to sing with swollen vocal folds is the easiest way to get hurt.

Build6. Chest-and-belly breathing

Hands on hips: feel the "rubber ring" around your waist

Singing breath rests on the lower belly muscles. It goes by many names: dantian breathing, diaphragmatic breathing, belly breathing. The point is always chest and belly working together.

Use a mirror or ask a neighbour to watch: if your shoulders rise, the air went into the top of your chest.

When you breathe in, which sign means the air went to the right place?

  1. Shoulders rise
  2. Chest puffs up
  3. The waist expands at the front, sides and back
  4. The belly pulls hard inward
Breathing in, the waist expands all the way round while shoulders and collarbones stay still; breathing out, the waist and belly slowly draw back in.

Turn8. Using the main muscles｜4/4 time, one breath per quarter note

Three muscle workouts, four bars each

/s/

Belly strength

Make a breathy sound; after four bars your belly should feel tight.

/hei/

Throat strength

Sing low notes to build control over low pitches.

/hu/

Combined workout

Alternate one bar of low notes and one bar of falsetto.

In quick in, quick out, if your inhale is noisy, you're using too much throat. Relax the throat and let the belly lead.

Turn9. Making your breath last longer

Make each breath go further

Quick in, slow out

When the /s/ exhale runs out, press lightly on your belly with your hand, then breathe in quickly.
Breathing out on /i/ instead also trains the belly muscles.

Quick in, hold, out

After a full breath, hold it and count, then breathe out.
Count a little longer each time.

  1. It gets louder
  2. Weak line endings, dragging

When you're short of breath, line endings go weak and drag, and you may even break in the middle of a word. Mark your breath points first, then sing.
