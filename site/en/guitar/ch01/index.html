Which instrument is most likely the guitar's ancestor?

Hint: it has a rounded back and a short neck, and people in the Middle East and North Africa still play it today.

  1. Harp
  2. Oud
  3. Violin
  4. Piano
When the oud reached Europe it became the lute, which slowly developed into the vihuela, the four-course and five-course guitars, and finally today's six-string guitar.

The big family of plucked strings

Pluck the strings with fingers or a pick and the wooden body resonates with them. Guitar, ukulele, mandolin and banjo all belong to this family.

Build00:20–00:55 Part 1: Where the guitar came from

From the oud of the Middle East all the way to Spain

What "course" means

Early guitars had strings in pairs, pressed and plucked together, a bit like today's 12-string guitar. Four courses means eight strings.

Spain's place

The vihuela and five-course guitar thrived most in Spain, and the classical guitar is still called the "Spanish guitar" today.

Six single strings

In the late 18th century the paired strings became six single strings, and the tuning settled on today's E A D G B E.

BuildPart 2: How the guitar changed (1)

The highs and lows of the classical guitar

1450–1600

Renaissance

The four-course guitar used gut strings. The five-course guitar appeared in the 16th century and gradually replaced it.

1600–1750

Baroque

The five-course guitar was popular at court and among ordinary people, often accompanying singing and dancing; the lute slowly left the stage.

About 1780–1850

The first golden age

The six-string guitar takes shape. Sor, Aguado and Carcassi left a large body of pieces and method books.

About 1850–1900

The Romantic low point

The piano took centre stage and fewer and fewer composers wrote for guitar. Tárrega brought the guitar back to audiences through his playing and arrangements.

The piano was loud, could play melody and harmony at once, and suited big concert halls; guitars of the time were quiet and hard to hear in a hall. "Not loud enough" comes up again and again in the guitar's history.

BuildPart 2: How the guitar changed (2)

For more volume: classical, folk and electric guitars

Late 19th century

The modern classical guitar

Spanish luthier Torres enlarged the body, set the string length at about 65 cm and used fan bracing, establishing what the classical guitar looks like today.

20th century

Into the concert hall

Segovia championed it all his life, and Rodrigo's Concierto de Aranjuez put the classical guitar on the concerto stage.

1920s–1930s

The folk guitar is born

Blues, country and folk rose in America. Martin switched to higher-tension steel strings and strengthened the body for a brighter, louder sound.

1930s–1950s

The electric guitar is born

Pickups turn string vibration into an electrical signal that an amp can make louder. In 1950 Leo Fender released a mass-produced solid-body electric guitar.

For three hundred years, nearly every change to the guitar has been about volume, so the guitar can be heard clearly in a band.

BuildPart 3: Kinds of guitar

The guitar family: check which kind you have

Classical guitar

Classical / Spanish

Nylon strings and a wide neck. Played solo with the fingers, with a focus on technique and staff notation.

Flamenco guitar

Flamenco

Looks like a classical guitar but lighter, with a bright, crisp sound; often played with dance and castanets.

Folk guitar

Folk / Acoustic

Steel strings, a bright tone and plenty of volume. Accompanies pop songs with chords and a capo, and can be fingerpicked too.

Electric guitar

Electric

Makes its sound through pickups and an amp. Split into lead and rhythm, with the electric bass handling the low end.

Hawaiian guitar

Lap Steel

Laid flat on the lap, the left hand slides a steel bar and the right hand wears finger picks, for a long, singing sound.

Relatives

Ukulele · Banjo · Mandolin

The four-string ukulele, the round-headed banjo and the mandolin, whose back looks like half a pear.

The air bounces back and forth inside the body, grows louder, and finally comes out of the sound hole. That's why the top and body wood affect the tone most.

Which part goes in the question-mark box?

  1. Nut

The other end of the string rests on the saddle, which sits in the bridge, which is glued to the top, so the vibration travels all the way into the top. The nut is at the neck end and sets the string spacing.

Turn01:05–01:40 Part 3: What the body is made of

Laminated and solid: both wood, each with its own tone

How to tell

Look at the edge of the top through the sound hole: if the top and bottom layers are different colours with a layer in between, it's most likely laminated; if the grain runs through in one piece, it's solid.

A "solid top" means only the top is solid; "all solid" means the sides and back are solid too.

The body wood decides the tone, resonance and volume. Choosing a guitar within your budget with sensible materials that's easy to press is more practical than chasing the finest wood.

TurnTop, sides and back

Common woods: each has its own colour and its own tone

SpruceTop

Pale yellow and hard. A bright, clear sound; the most common top on folk guitars.

CedarTop

Dark orange-brown and soft. A warm, round sound that vibrates easily; often used on classical guitars.

MahoganyBack and sides

Thick, warm mids and a focused tone; great for playing and singing.

RosewoodBack and sides

Deep lows, bright highs and rich overtones, with a sense of space.

MapleBack and sides

Hard and light, with a clean, direct sound that doesn't get buried by other instruments in a band.

KoaTop / back and sides

From Hawaii; the more it's played, the sweeter it sounds, with lovely upper mids.

WalnutBack and sides

Between mahogany and rosewood, a balanced, warm sound.

BasswoodElectric guitars

Light, soft and affordable, with gentle mids; common on electric guitars.

TurnPart 4: Size

Choose a size to fit your body; if it's comfortable to hold, you'll keep practising

  • Sitting with the guitar, your right arm rests naturally on the top edge of the body
  • With your left arm straight, your fingers reach fret 1 at the headstock end
  • Smaller adults can choose a GC or a travel guitar
  • When you try one: low action makes pressing comfortable
TurnPart 5: Inside the guitar

The parts you can't see: bracing and the truss rod

Bracing is the wooden strips glued under the top. It takes the pull of the strings and shapes how the top vibrates. Steel strings pull hard, so folk guitars use stronger X bracing.

Myth or fact? Guess with a show of hands, then flip the card

Put steel strings on a classical guitar and it gets louder
MythSteel strings pull too hard for a classical guitar's neck and bridge, which will lift or warp.
The bigger the guitar, the better for beginners
MythChoose by your body size. If the body is too big your right arm can't wrap around it, and your shoulder will hurt after a while.
Folk guitars are louder than classical guitars
FactHigher-tension steel strings plus X bracing give a bright, loud sound.
Solid-wood guitars sound better the more you play them
FactA single piece of wood vibrating over years sounds more and more open; laminates change less.
If pressing hurts your fingers, the guitar is broken
MythSore fingertips for the first two weeks are normal; if the action at string 6, fret 12 is over about 3 mm, have a technician adjust it.
It's fine to leave the guitar in the car or in the sun by a window after playing
MythHeat and humidity make wood swell or crack. Keep it in its bag out of the sun, and dehumidify in Taiwan's summers.

Close01:40–02:00 Tuning the guitar

Standard tuning: E A D G B E

  • From thick to thin: string 6 E, string 5 A, string 4 D, string 3 G, string 2 B, string 1 E
  • Neighbouring strings are a perfect fourth apart; only strings 3 to 2 are a major third
  • Strings 1 and 6 are both E, two octaves apart
  • Say it: Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie

Before tuning, check which way the peg turns to "tighten". Tightening raises the pitch and loosening lowers it.
