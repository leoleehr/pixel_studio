OpenThink about it

C and Cm sound very different. How many notes apart are they?

C = C, E, G. Which option is Cm?

  1. Only one note different: E becomes Eb
  2. All three notes are different
  3. It has one extra note
  4. A different root
Major and minor triads differ only in the middle "third": swap the major third for a minor third and the sound turns from bright to sad.

The original handbook's prelude

Book 2 starts with "intervals": an interval is the distance in pitch between two notes, measured in "degrees". That's where we start today.

Counting degrees

Count both ends: C to E passes through the letter names C, D and E, so it's a "third".

Telling major from minor

Both are thirds, but C–E is 4 semitones (a major third) and A–C is 3 semitones (a minor third).

On the guitar

One fret is one semitone. Count frets up from any fret and you have the number of semitones.

BuildConsonance and dissonance

Some notes sound settled together, some sound tense

Perfect consonance

Unisons, fourths, fifths and octaves. Open and stable, like a building's frame.

Imperfect consonance

Major and minor thirds and sixths. Sweet and colourful; they give a chord its colour.

Dissonance

Major and minor seconds and sevenths, and the augmented fourth. Tense and restless; this is where music's tension comes from.

Dissonance gives music a feeling of "wanting to resolve", like the conflict in a story. The dominant 7th chord in Chapter 10 relies on one dissonant note to push the music home.

Listen to two notes and tell how far apart they are

  • Octave: the two notes sound like one note, just higher and lower
  • Fifth: open and solid, rock's power chord
  • Fourth: like asking a question; neighbouring open strings on the guitar are fourths
  • Major third: bright; minor third: sad

BuildSuspensions and dominant 7ths

A suspended sound has to come home

  • Sus4: swap the third for a fourth and the sound hangs in mid-air; switch back to the major triad and it settles
  • D → Dsus4 → D takes just the little finger, and lots of intros are played this way
  • G7: a G chord plus the dissonant F, pulling strongly back to C
  • Listen closely to both "hanging → home" feelings

Turn01:05–01:40 Chapter 1: Fingerstyle technique

Put down the pick: each of four fingers looks after its own strings

  • Thumb T: strings 4, 5 and 6, playing the chord's bass
  • Index 1, middle 2, ring 3: strings 3, 2 and 1
  • Wrist slightly arched, fingers curling in toward the palm like hooks
  • The thumb pushes down and forward, keeping clear of the other fingers
  • Nails a little long (about 1 mm) give a brighter sound

TurnPattern 1

T-1-2-3: the most basic arpeggio

Hold the chord with your left hand, and play up the strings with your right in the order "thumb, index, middle, ring". The thumb's bass changes with the chord: C on string 5, Am on open string 5, F on string 4, G on string 6.

T-1-2-1-3-1-2-1: up, then back down

Eight notes spread evenly across a bar. The index finger plays most often and keeps time for the whole pattern.

Close01:40–02:00 Thinking about fingerpicking

Myth or fact?

You must grow long nails to fingerpick
MythFinger pads work too, with a warmer sound; a little nail sounds brighter.
When fingerpicking, the left hand can press only the strings you use
MythHold the whole chord first so the right hand never lands on an empty note.
The thumb only plays bass
FactThe thumb covers the bass on strings 4–6, and the whole pattern's timing rests on it.
Minor chords are just "lower" chords
MythThey're not necessarily lower; the middle third is a semitone smaller, so it sounds sadder.
Dissonant intervals should be avoided
MythDissonance creates tension, and lots of great songs are driven by "tension → resolution".
Fingerpicking goes slow first, then fast
FactGet every note even in volume and accurate, then speed up gradually.
