BuildWhat chords do

At home, setting out, wanting to go home

Tonic TI, vi, iii · at home
Subdominant SIV, ii · heading out
Dominant DV, vii° · wants to go home
Tonic TI · back home

Tonic

Sounds like a full stop. Songs usually start and end here.

Subdominant

Leaving home, with only a little tension. Often the first chord of a chorus.

Dominant

The most tense, the most eager to resolve. Follow V with I and your ears breathe a sigh of relief.

V7 → I: the strongest signal to go home

G7

E7
A7
  • G7 = a G chord plus F. F and B form a dissonant augmented fourth that badly wants to resolve
  • B moves up to C and F moves down to E, landing on a C chord
  • Add a seventh to V in any key and it becomes a "dominant 7th"
  • In a song's last line, swap V for V7 and the ending hits harder

Relative major and minor

C major and A minor use the same chords; only where "home" is differs. G major's relative minor is E minor.

Try it

Swap the F in C → G → Am → F for Dm: C → G → Am → Dm sounds softer, with more depth.

Myth or fact?

Using the same progression is plagiarism
MythChord progressions are shared material; a song's character comes from its melody and lyrics.
Scale degrees make changing key easy
FactRemember I–V–vi–IV and you can play it straight away in any key.
Seventh chords are only for jazz
MythPop ballads use lots of maj7 and m7 to soften the sound.
V must always be followed by I
MythV to vi is called a "deceptive cadence"; it creates an unexpected turn, and lots of choruses use it.
Songs in minor keys are always sad
MythFast songs in minor keys can sound powerful and cool too.
When putting chords to a melody, look at the strong-beat notes first
FactStrong-beat notes decide the chord most; weak-beat notes can be passing notes.
